In 1665, the astronomer Cassini saw a dark oval on Jupiter. It was probably not the Great Red Spot.
Astronomers kept reporting his spot until 1713.
Then, for about a hundred and eighteen years, nobody recorded it at all.
The storm we call the Great Red Spot first shows up in drawings in 1831.
And Cassini's spot was only a third to a half as long as the Great Red Spot was in 1879.
To be the same storm, it would have had to keep growing for more than a century, unseen.
No storm on Jupiter has ever been seen to grow like that.
So the Great Red Spot is nearly two hundred years old. Cassini's spot was most likely a different storm, long gone.

SOURCES (for approval — every claim in the script, with the number behind it)

Primary paper, read at the publisher on 2026-11-17 (agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com,
fetched through the Firecrawl scraper because this sandbox cannot reach it
directly): Sánchez-Lavega, García-Melendo, Legarreta, Miró, Soria &
Ahrens-Velásquez, "The Origin of Jupiter's Great Red Spot", Geophysical Research
Letters 51(12), e2024GL108993 (2024), doi:10.1029/2024GL108993. Called "SL24"
below.

1. "In 1665, the astronomer Cassini saw a dark oval on Jupiter."
   SL24, abstract: "Cassini discovered in 1665 the presence of a dark oval at
   the GRS latitude, known as the 'Permanent Spot' (PS)". Body: observed by
   "D. Cassini and other astronomers in July-September 1665".

2. "It was probably not the Great Red Spot."
   SL24, abstract: "PS is unlikely to correspond to the current GRS". "Probably
   not" carries the paper's "unlikely"; the script does not say "was not".

3. "Astronomers kept reporting his spot until 1713."
   SL24: "PS was subsequently observed by Cassini and others in 1667, 1672,
   1677, 1685-1687, 1690-1691, 1694, 1708, and was last reported in 1713 by
   M. Maraldi".

4. "Then, for about a hundred and eighteen years, nobody recorded it at all."
   SL24: "No reports of PS or any sign of its presence exists in the available
   observations of Jupiter between 1713 and 1831, a long period of ~118 years".
   "Nobody recorded it" = no report in the surviving observations, which is
   what the paper says; it does not say nobody looked.

5. "The storm we call the Great Red Spot first shows up in drawings in 1831."
   SL24: "The first drawings showing the signature of the current GRS, recorded
   by its Hollow, date back to 1831". Abstract: "the current GRS, that was first
   observed in 1831".

6. "Cassini's spot was only a third to a half as long as the Great Red Spot was
   in 1879."
   SL24: "The length of PS is 2-3 times smaller than that of the 1879 GRS."
   2-3 times smaller = one third to one half the length.

7. "To be the same storm, it would have had to keep growing for more than a
   century, unseen. No storm on Jupiter has ever been seen to grow like that."
   SL24: "PS would have had to grow steadily from 1713 to 1879 at a rate of
   ~+0.14°/year (160 km/year) to be the GRS. This is highly unlikely since ...
   no reports of PS or GRS exist during this long period and, in addition, no
   continuously sustained grow in size has been never reported in Jupiter's
   vortices". 1713 to 1879 is 166 years, so "more than a century".

8. "So the Great Red Spot is nearly two hundred years old."
   SL24: "The current GRS has therefore certainly been in existence for 193
   years" (written in 2024, counting from 1831). From 1831 to 2026 is 195
   years: "nearly two hundred". This is a minimum (first record), and the
   script calls it the age because that is the recorded age; it does not claim
   the storm formed in 1831.

9. "Cassini's spot was most likely a different storm, long gone."
   SL24: "it is most likely that this lack of observations during such a large
   period means that PS disappeared."

Not used: a 1711 painting showing the PS "with a red tint" (SL24). It is an
interesting wrinkle but does not change the conclusion, and the paper only
calls it intriguing.
