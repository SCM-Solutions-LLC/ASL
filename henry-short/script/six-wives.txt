The fourth wife beat them all.

Henry VIII had six wives, and the one he couldn't wait to get rid of outlived
every single one.

Wife one, Catherine of Aragon. 24 years of marriage, annulled. Henry broke
England away from Rome to do it.

Wife two, Anne Boleyn. She gave him a daughter, the future Elizabeth I. Three
years in, Henry had her executed.

Wife three, Jane Seymour. She finally gave him a male heir who lived. She died
12 days later.

Wife four, Anne of Cleves. Henry met her and wanted out. Six months later,
annulled, but she walked away with a palace, a manor, and a new title: the
King's sister.

Wife five, Catherine Howard. Executed.

Wife six, Catherine Parr. She outlived Henry.

Divorced, beheaded, died. Divorced, beheaded, survived. But here's what the
rhyme leaves out. Anne of Cleves outlived Henry and all five other wives. The
wife he rejected was the last one standing.

1. "The fourth wife beat them all." / "Henry VIII had six wives, and the one
   he couldn't wait to get rid of outlived every single one."
   The hook (A4). True as stated: claims 5 and 8. "Six wives": Nash 1789,
   p. 1, "Katharine Parr, sixth and last wife of Henry the Eighth". "Couldn't
   wait to get rid of": claim 5 (he considered breaking off before the
   wedding; annulled after six months). "Outlived every single one": claim 8.
   "Beat them all" is the short's figure of speech for "outlived them all",
   explained at once by the next sentence.

2. "Wife one, Catherine of Aragon. 24 years of marriage, annulled."
   Married June 1509: Wriothesley vol. 1, p. 6, "And in June followinge the
   King was married to Queene Katherin, late wife of his brother Prince
   Arthure". Annulled 23 May 1533: L&P vol. 6, no. 528, Cranmer to Henry,
   "Today, 23 May, I have given sentence in your great and weighty cause";
   no. 526, Bedyll to Cromwell, "Sentence of divorce was given this day, 23
   May". June 1509 to May 1533 is 23 years 11 months; "24 years" rounds it.

3. "Henry broke England away from Rome to do it."
   Act in Restraint of Appeals, 24 Hen. VIII c. 12 (1533), Statutes vol. 3:
   "Causes of Matrimony and Divorces ... shalbe frome hensforth harde
   examined ... and det'myned within the Kinges Jurisdiccion and Auctoritie
   and not elleswhere". L&P vol. 6, editor's preface, pp. xvii-xviii: Parliament
   was "compelled to pass the measures devised by the King to cut off papal
   interference in the matter of his divorce and marriage. It was enacted that
   appeals to Rome in ecclesiastical causes should no longer be allowed." The
   break had other causes too; the script says only that he did it to end the
   marriage, which this supports.

4. "Wife two, Anne Boleyn. She gave him a daughter, the future Elizabeth I.
   Three years in, Henry had her executed."
   Daughter: Wriothesley vol. 1, p. 22, "the viith daie of September, 1533
   ... Queene Anne was brought to bedd of a faire daughter"; p. 23, named
   "Elizabeth"; editor's note, "The Princess Elizabeth, afterwards Queen of
   England". Marriage: L&P vol. 6, no. 661, Cranmer, 17 June 1533, "her
   marriage, which took place about St. Paul's day last" (25 January 1533;
   Wriothesley's editor notes 14 November 1532 as the other date given).
   Executed: Wriothesley vol. 1, p. 41, "the 19th day of May, 1536 ... Anne
   Bulleyn, Queene, was brought to execution". About three years and four
   months (or three and a half) after either wedding date: "three years in".
   "Had her executed": she was tried and condemned for treason; the script
   makes no claim about the charges.

5. "Wife three, Jane Seymour. She finally gave him a male heir who lived. She
   died 12 days later."
   L&P vol. 12 pt 2, no. 889, Queen Jane's letter announcing "the birth of her
   son ... Hampton Court, 12 Oct." (1537); no. 1060, "Queen Jane, mother of
   Edward VI., who died at Hampton Court, 24 Oct." 12 to 24 October is 12
   days. (Wriothesley's text gives "the 14 of October"; his editor corrects
   it to the 24th. The script follows L&P.) "A male heir who lived": Edward,
   who became king (no. 1060, "Queen Jane, mother of Edward VI."). Catherine
   of Aragon had borne a son who died in infancy (Wriothesley vol. 1, A.D.
   1511: "Prince Henrie, the Kings first sonne, was borne at Richmonde on Newe
   Yeares daye, and on St. Mathie's day after the saide Prince died"); hence
   "finally" and "who lived". (An earlier wording, "finally gave him a son", failed the
   fact-check over the 1511 prince.)

6. "Wife four, Anne of Cleves. Henry met her and wanted out. Six months
   later, annulled"
   Met: Wriothesley vol. 1, p. 109, "on New Year's daie at afternoune the
   Kinges Grace ... came privelie to Rochester" (1 January 1540). Wanted out:
   L&P vol. 15, no. 825, the King's own declaration, "when he saw her for the
   first time at Rochester ... he liked her so ill he was sorry she had come
   and he considered if it were possible to break off"; no. 823, Cromwell to
   Henry, the King's words, "Nothing so well as she was spoken of".
   Married: Wriothesley vol. 1, p. 111, "on Twelfe daie ... the Kinges
   Majestie was maried to the said Queene Anne" (6 January 1540). Annulled:
   L&P vol. 15, no. 860, Convocation, "they have found the marriage null ...
   Westm., 9 July 1540"; confirmed by statute, 32 Hen. VIII c. 25, "the
   mariage betwene his Highnes and the said lady Anne of Cleves is clerely
   voide". 6 January to 9 July: six months. (An earlier wording, "met her
   once", was dropped: he met her many times.)

7. "but she walked away with a palace, a manor, and a new title: the King's
   sister."
   L&P vol. 15, no. 925, Henry to Anne, 12 July 1540: "We have appointed you
   two houses, that at Richemont where you now lie, and the other at
   Blechinglegh" (Richmond Palace; the manor of Bletchingley, Surrey), and he
   is "content to repute you as our dearest sister"; her household was sworn
   "to serve her as the King's sister"; no. 899, "She will be considered as
   the King's sister". She signed herself "your Majesty's most humble sister".
   "Title" is the plain word for that style. (An earlier wording, "a castle"
   and "the King's beloved sister", was dropped: Hever appears only in an
   18th-century county history, and "beloved" only in Henry's salutation.)

8. "Wife five, Catherine Howard. Executed."
   Wriothesley vol. 1, p. 134: "the 13th of February, beinge Monday, the sayd
   Quene, Katharine Haward, was beheaded within the Tower on the grene" (1542).

9. "Wife six, Catherine Parr. She outlived Henry."
   Henry died 28 January 1547: Wriothesley vol. 1, p. 178, "the Kinges
   Majestie Henrie the Eight ... deceased ... on Fridaie last, being the 28th
   of Januarie". Parr died 5 September 1548: Nash 1789, p. 2, quoting the
   heralds' record, "on Wenysdaye the 5 Septembre ... died the aforesaid
   Ladye, late Quene Dowager, at the Castle of Sudley".

10. "Divorced, beheaded, died. Divorced, beheaded, survived."
   A traditional schoolroom rhyme, quoted as a rhyme, not as a claim; no
   citable origin was found and none is claimed. "Divorced" is the rhyme's
   word for the two annulments; contemporaries used it too (claim 2,
   "Sentence of divorce").

11. "But here's what the rhyme leaves out. Anne of Cleves outlived Henry and
   all five other wives. The wife he rejected was the last one standing."
   Anne died in July 1557 at Chelsea: Wriothesley vol. 2, p. 138, "This yeare
   in Julie died the Ladie Ann of Cleve, at Chelsey ... the 5 of August her
   corps were solemnly brought from thence to the Abbey of Westminster";
   Machyn, p. 145, "The iij day of August my lade Anne of Cleyff ... cam from
   Chelsey to be [buried] unto Westmynster". (The exact day, 16 July, is
   supplied by both editors, not the original texts; the script gives no
   day.) The other five died by 1548: Catherine of Aragon 1536 (Wriothesley
   vol. 1, p. 33), Anne Boleyn 1536 (claim 4), Jane Seymour 1537 (claim 5),
   Catherine Howard 1542 (claim 8), Catherine Parr 1548 (claim 9). Henry
   1547 (claim 9). So she was the last of the six. "Rejected": claim 6.
