Jupiter is the biggest planet in the solar system.
Two and a half times the mass of every other planet combined.
But hold Jupiter up next to the Sun, and it looks tiny.
It would take nearly a thousand Jupiters to fill the Sun.
And here is the part that gets people.
Our Sun is not an average star. It is an unusually big one.
It is bigger and brighter than over ninety percent of the stars in our part of the galaxy,
because most stars are red dwarfs: small, dim, and everywhere.
So look at the nearest star to our own.
Proxima Centauri, just over four light years away.
The closest star to the Sun, and you have almost certainly never seen it.
It is almost always too faint for your eyes.
That is what most of the stars around us look like.
The star we call ordinary is one of the rare big ones.

SOURCES (for approval — every claim in the script, with the number behind it)

1. "Jupiter is the biggest planet in the solar system. Two and a half times the
   mass of every other planet combined."
   NASA Planetary Fact Sheet (https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/):
   Jupiter 1898e24 kg, the largest mass and diameter of the eight planets. The other
   seven: 0.330 + 4.87 + 5.97 + 0.642 + 568 + 86.8 + 102 = 768.6e24 kg. Ratio 2.47.
   On screen: "2.5× the other planets combined, by mass".

2. "Hold Jupiter up next to the Sun, and it looks tiny."
   NASA Sun and Jupiter fact sheets (.../factsheet/sunfact.html,
   .../factsheet/jupiterfact.html): mean radius 695,700 km against 69,911 km, so
   Jupiter is about a tenth of the Sun's width.

3. "It would take nearly a thousand Jupiters to fill the Sun."
   Same fact sheets: Sun volume 1,412,000e12 km^3, Jupiter 1,431.28e12 km^3. Ratio
   986.5, so "nearly a thousand", and a full thousand would overflow it. This is
   volume (melted down and poured in); solid spheres pack worse. By mass the Sun is
   ~1,048 Jupiters, a different and coincidentally similar number.
   On screen: "≈ 1,000 JUPITERS / to fill it".

4. "Our Sun is not an average star. It is an unusually big one. It is bigger and
   brighter than over ninety percent of the stars in our part of the galaxy."
   Reylé et al., "The 10 parsec sample in the Gaia era", A&A 650, A201 (2021),
   doi:10.1051/0004-6361/202140985, Table 3 (Sun excluded): A 4, F 8, G 18, K 38,
   M 249, white dwarfs 20, plus 41 untyped (36 of them possible M dwarfs). K and M
   dwarfs are all smaller and dimmer than the Sun: Boyajian et al., "Stellar
   Diameters and Temperatures II. Main Sequence K & M Stars", ApJ 757, 112 (2012),
   doi:10.1088/0004-637X/757/2/112 (arXiv:1208.2431), Table 3: interferometric
   radii and luminosities of K0 V to M4 V dwarfs, the largest the K0 V stars
   GJ 702A (0.831 R_sun, 0.53 L_sun), GJ 75 (0.819, 0.52) and GJ 631 (0.759,
   0.42); every M dwarf in the table is under 0.58 R_sun and 0.07 L_sun. White dwarfs are all far smaller and dimmer than the Sun: Giammichele,
   Bergeron & Dufour, "Know Your Neighborhood: A Detailed Model Atmosphere
   Analysis of Nearby White Dwarfs", ApJS 199, 29 (2012),
   doi:10.1088/0067-0049/199/2/29 (arXiv:1202.5581), Table 2 gives Teff and
   log L/L_sun for all 165 white dwarfs within 20 pc. The brightest is log L =
   -1.12 (0.08 L_sun); within 10 pc the brightest is Sirius B (WD 0642-166),
   log L = -1.53 (0.03 L_sun). Radius from those two columns by Stefan-Boltzmann,
   R = sqrt(L) x (5772 K / Teff)^2: none exceeds 0.022 R_sun. Even counting
   every G star as at least as big as the Sun (they are not; Tau Ceti and others
   are late G), the Sun is bigger than about 91% of the typed stars; counting only
   the G0-G2 and evolved G stars, about 95%. The exception among the K stars is
   the subgiant delta Eridani (K0 IV, in the Reylé catalogue, CDS
   J/A+A/650/A201), whose interferometric radius is larger than the Sun's
   (Thévenin et al., A&A 436, 253 (2005), doi:10.1051/0004-6361:20042075);
   counting it as well the worst case is 306 of 337, 90.8%. "Over ninety percent"
   holds under every reading, with that thin margin. The census is of the solar neighbourhood, which is why the script
   says "our part of the galaxy" rather than "the galaxy".
   On screen: "THE SUN / bigger than 9 stars in 10".

5. "Because most stars are red dwarfs: small, dim, and everywhere."
   Reylé et al. 2021, as above: 249 M dwarfs of 337 typed stars, 74%. Small and
   dim: Boyajian et al. 2012 Table 3 (claim 4), M dwarfs 0.19-0.58 R_sun and
   0.003-0.07 L_sun. The paper's
   own figure is 58.9 ± 5.8% of all typed objects including brown dwarfs. Every
   reading is a majority.

6. "So look at the nearest star to our own. Proxima Centauri, just over four light
   years away. The closest star to the Sun."
   Anglada-Escudé et al., Nature 536, 437 (2016), doi:10.1038/nature19106:
   Proxima Centauri, at 1.295 pc, "is the Sun's closest stellar neighbour". NASA
   Exoplanet Archive (pscomppars, Proxima Cen): 1.30119 ± 0.00034 pc = 4.244 ly,
   so "just over four light years". The script says "closest star to the Sun", not "closest
   star there is", because the closest star to us is the Sun.

7. "You have almost certainly never seen it. It is almost always too faint for
   your eyes."
   NASA Exoplanet Archive: V = 11.01. Howard et al., ApJL 860, L30 (2018),
   doi:10.3847/2041-8213/aacaf3, report "the first naked-eye-brightness superflare
   detected from Proxima Centauri" (March 2016, a 68x brightening), which is only
   remarkable because its normal brightness is far below what the eye can see.
   That flare is why both lines are hedged with "almost". A viewer with a telescope
   may have seen it, hence "almost certainly".

8. "That is what most of the stars around us look like."
   Proxima is M5.5 V (NASA Exoplanet Archive), an M dwarf. Reylé et al. 2021: M
   dwarfs are 74% of the typed stars within 10 pc, so "most of the stars around
   us" are M dwarfs like it. Scoped to "around us" because the census is of the
   solar neighbourhood.

9. "The star we call ordinary is one of the rare big ones."
   Reylé et al. 2021, as in claim 4. K and M dwarfs and white dwarfs are all
   smaller than the Sun (claim 4); everything else, the Sun's own class G and the
   hotter A and F stars, is A 4 + F 8 + G 18 + the Sun = 31 of 379 stars (337
   typed + 41 untyped + the Sun), about 8%. "Rare big ones" means that group.
   On screen: a grid of 100 dots by spectral class, per 100 of the 379: 8 large
   bright (G or hotter, 31), 10 orange (K, 38), 5 tiny white (white dwarfs, 20),
   77 red (M 249 + 41 untyped, 36 of which Reylé lists as possible M dwarfs).
   The dots show class, not size, and the screen says so ("dots: star type, not
   size"); then "THE SUN / one of the few".

10. Not spoken: "PROXIMA CENTAURI / ≈ 1/7 the Sun's radius".
   Sources disagree (A3). Measured: the interferometric radius 0.141 ± 0.007 R_sun
   (Demory et al. 2009, as given in Kervella et al., A&A 598, L7 (2017),
   doi:10.1051/0004-6361/201629930), and Anglada-Escudé et al. 2016 quote a
   "measured radius of 0.14". Model-based: Kervella 2017 adopt 0.1542 ± 0.0045
   from a mass-radius relation. NASA Exoplanet Archive: 0.141 ± 0.021 (Suárez
   Mascareño et al. 2025, 2025A&A...700A..11M). The label uses the measured value,
   1/7 = 0.143, inside the measured and Archive ranges; Proxima is drawn at 13.7
   against the Sun's 96.

11. Not spoken: the row of the other seven planets beside Jupiter, and Jupiter
   beside the Sun.
   Drawn to one scale with Jupiter, at volumetric mean radii over Jupiter's
   69,911 km (NASA per-planet fact sheets,
   https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/<planet>fact.html): Saturn 58,232 km
   (0.833), Uranus 25,362 (0.363), Neptune 24,622 (0.352), Earth 6,371 (0.091),
   Venus 6,051.8 (0.087), Mars 3,389.5 (0.0485), Mercury 2,439.7 (0.035).
   Beside the Sun, Jupiter is drawn at 52 against the Sun's 520 (true ratio 9.95,
   claim 2). The 1,000-Jupiter field is labelled "by volume" and "dots not to
   scale": a flat disc holds only ~99 Jupiter-sized discs, so the dots are drawn
   smaller than Jupiter to show a volume count.
