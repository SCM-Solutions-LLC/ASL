In 2024, Stonehenge's Altar Stone was traced to Scotland. Two years on, scientists still can't agree where it came from.
The ages of tiny mineral grains inside it matched sandstone in the northeast.
If so, how did six tonnes travel more than seven hundred kilometres?
Clarke and colleagues, who traced it, suggested it went by sea.
But no late Stone Age boat has been found in Britain, and a study by Parker Pearson and colleagues doubts one could carry six tonnes.
Overland, on a sledge, they estimate about eight months of hauling.
A glacier? A 2026 ice model by Clarke and colleagues says ice could have carried it part of the way, at most.
Then, months later, a 2026 study by Pirrie and colleagues, including three of the 2024 team, looked closer.
Many outcrops in that part of Scotland could not have been the source. No outcrop examined so far has been shown to match it in every way.
Six tonnes, from a spot no one has pinned down, by a route no one knows. Sea, sledge or ice: how do you think it got there?

Pirrie 2026 also notes (Discussion) that the 2024 grain ages "do not exclude
a potential source for the Altar Stone from the Carboniferous", but adds that
the isotope data "indicate an ORS provenance is more likely than a
Carboniferous source". The script names no rock unit beyond what claims 1, 2
and 9 say, so neither reading is contradicted.

The two 2026 papers disagree about the Scottish attribution itself. Clarke
2026 still favours northeast Scotland: Caithness sandstone gives "the closest
match in zircon age structure", though "its source location within this large
basin remains unresolved". Pirrie 2026 (three of whose authors wrote Clarke
2024) questions it: its Introduction says "detailed multi-proxy
characterization of the Altar Stone raises questions over this
interpretation" (the Orcadian Basin source); its Discussion, that scarce chrome
spinel "may indicate that an origin from this part of NE Scotland needs
re-evaluating", that ORS areas "from the Scottish Midland Valley to the
Anglo-Welsh borderland ... remain possible source areas from a heavy mineral
standpoint", and that the 2024 grain ages do "not exclude a potential source
for the Altar Stone from the Carboniferous" (while isotopes make "an ORS
provenance ... more likely than a Carboniferous source"); its Conclusions,
"the provenance of the Altar Stone has yet to be determined with confidence".
Per GUARDRAILS A3 the script makes only the claim both support: the source is
not settled, and the experts disagree. It does not say the stone is not from
Scotland (neither paper says so), and it does not say it is.

1. "In 2024, Stonehenge's Altar Stone was traced to Scotland. Two years on,
   scientists still can't agree where it came from."
   The hook (A4). Both sentences are true as stated, and a viewer who swipes
   away at the second leaves knowing the 2024 result is disputed, not settled.
   First: Clarke 2024, abstract, "Detrital age comparisons ... reveal a
   remarkable similarity to the Old Red Sandstone of the Orcadian Basin in
   northeast Scotland"; title, "A Scottish provenance for the Altar Stone of
   Stonehenge" (published 14 August 2024). Second: the disagreement above,
   between Clarke 2026 (June 2026) and Pirrie 2026 (November 2026), about two
   years after Clarke 2024. "Still" is accurate because no source has been
   agreed at any point: Parker Pearson 2024 (p.129), "As yet, the actual
   outcrop from which the Altar Stone was extracted is unknown"; Clarke 2026,
   "remains unresolved"; Pirrie 2026, "yet to be determined with confidence".
   (Earlier wordings, "Now nobody can say exactly" and "Exactly where it came
   from is still unknown", failed fact-checks: the first implied the spot had
   once been known, the second that Scotland was settled.) Not said: the name
   "Altar Stone" is conventional (Stone 80). No one knows it was an altar.

2. "The ages of tiny mineral grains inside it matched sandstone in the
   northeast."
   Clarke 2024: "we present in situ U-Pb, Lu-Hf and trace element isotopic data
   for zircon, apatite and rutile from two fragments of the Altar Stone"; the
   grains are "typically 10-40 µm wide"; abstract as in claim 1 ("northeast
   Scotland").

3. "If so, how did six tonnes travel more than seven hundred kilometres?"
   Clarke 2024, abstract: "Such a provenance implies that the Altar Stone, a 6
   tonne shaped block, was sourced at least 750 km from its current location";
   body: "measuring 4.9 x 1.0 x 0.5 m ... weighing 6 t". "More than seven
   hundred" sits under Clarke 2024's "at least 750 km". Clarke 2026 gives a
   rounder "some 700 km" ("the 6000 kg central sandstone megalith"), which the
   script does not contradict. The distance holds only for an Orcadian Basin
   source, as Clarke 2024 frames it ("Such a provenance implies"); "If so"
   keeps that condition, since claim 1 says the source is
   unresolved. "When" is unknown:
   Parker Pearson 2024, "Just when the Altar Stone arrived at Stonehenge is
   unknown"; Pirrie 2026 abstract, "Its purpose at the monument, and when it
   was transported to the site, is unknown". So the script gives no date for
   the journey.

4. "Clarke and colleagues, who traced it, suggested it went by sea."
   "Clarke and colleagues" = Clarke 2024 (first author Anthony J. I. Clarke,
   per Crossref); "who traced it" = claim 1.
   Clarke 2024, abstract: "The difficulty of long-distance overland transport
   of such massive cargo from Scotland, navigating topographic barriers,
   suggests that it was transported by sea."

5. "But no late Stone Age boat has been found in Britain, and a study by
   Parker Pearson and colleagues doubts one could carry six tonnes."
   Parker Pearson 2024, "How was the Altar Stone transported?": "Although no
   boats are known from the Late Neolithic in Britain or Europe ..."; "The
   earliest known boats in Britain have been found ... at Ferriby. The earliest
   of these Early Bronze Age sewn-plank boats (Ferriby 3) dates to 2030-1780
   cal bc"; "What we know of Early Bronze Age boats ... would suggest that they
   and their Late Neolithic precursors were unlikely to be sturdy enough to
   carry a 6-tonne stone in coastal waters." "One" = a boat of that period
   (the Early Bronze Age boats "and their Late Neolithic precursors"). "Late Stone Age" is the plain
   rendering of "Late Neolithic" (Neolithic = New Stone Age). "Doubts" matches
   "unlikely". "Parker Pearson and colleagues" = Parker Pearson 2024 (first
   author Mike Parker Pearson); three of its six authors (Bevins, Ixer,
   Pearce) also wrote Clarke 2024, and the script does not call it
   independent.

6. "Overland, on a sledge, they estimate about eight months of hauling."
   "They" = Parker Pearson and colleagues (claim 5).
   Parker Pearson 2024: "necessitating the use of a suitably robust wooden
   sledge ... sliding on wooden 'rails'"; "such a journey with a large and
   heavy sledge could take perhaps four times today's time of under two months
   ... needing eight months or so to haul a stone from northern Scotland to
   Stonehenge." "About" stands for "perhaps ... or so": it is an estimate, and
   the script says so.

7. "A glacier? A 2026 ice model by Clarke and colleagues says ice could have
   carried it part of the way, at most."
   "Clarke and colleagues" = Clarke 2026, whose first author (Anthony J. I.
   Clarke, same ORCID per Crossref) also led Clarke 2024.
   Clarke 2026, abstract: "we combine sandstone provenance analysis with ice
   flow modelling"; "Glacial dispersal to Dogger Bank would reduce the
   anthropogenic transport distance required to Stonehenge from 700 km to 400
   km"; "Glacial transport may have provided an intermediate stage in the
   stone's journey, but alone cannot account for the final emplacement on
   Salisbury Plain. Even under a glacially assisted scenario, substantial
   anthropogenic transport would have remained necessary." "Part of the way, at
   most" = "may have provided an intermediate stage ... alone cannot account".
   The abstract adds a further problem (Dogger Bank
   was flooded before the stone likely arrived); the full text's discussion
   says the same: glacial transport "provides only an intermediate stage in
   the stone's history, reducing the overall anthropogenic transport distance
   but not removing the need for substantial later human agency". The script leaves the flooding out
   because it only weakens the glacier idea further. Parker Pearson 2024 goes
   further, "Clarke et al. (2024) have discussed why theories involving Ice Age
   glacial transport can be rejected", but the script uses the narrower 2026
   wording, which is based on an actual ice model.

8. "Then, months later, a 2026 study by Pirrie and colleagues, including
   three of the 2024 team, looked closer."
   Pirrie 2026 (Journal of the Geological Society, November 2026; Crossref
   gives the issue date 2026-11; first author Duncan Pirrie) came about five months after Clarke 2026 (4 June
   2026). Its authors include Bevins, Ixer and Pearce, all three of whom are
   authors of Clarke 2024. "Looked closer" stands for the abstract's "detailed
   multi-proxy characterization of the sedimentology, geochemistry, detrital
   and diagenetic mineralogy of the Altar Stone".

9. "Many outcrops in that part of Scotland could not have been the source. No
   outcrop examined so far has been shown to match it in every way."
   Pirrie 2026, abstract: "the overall petrology, heavy mineral assemblage, clay
   mineralogy and geochemistry show that many outcrops of the Old Red Sandstone
   (ORS) in the Orcadian Basin could not have been the source of the Altar
   Stone. To date, no examined outcrop area of the UK ORS can be shown to have
   the same suite of lithological and geochemical characteristics as
   documented for the Altar Stone." "Has been shown to" follows the abstract's "can be shown to have": no match
   has been demonstrated. "In every way" stands for "the same suite
   of ... characteristics": the grain ages still match (claim 2; Clarke 2024
   found apatite ages from Caithness and Orkney samples "match those from the
   Altar Stone"), but no outcrop matches the whole set. "That part of Scotland" = the Orcadian
   Basin of northeast Scotland (claim 2). The same abstract says the earlier
   grain dating "supports a Laurentian source for the heavy minerals", but the
   body goes further and questions the NE Scotland source itself (see the
   disagreement above the claims). The script says only what is above. The full text's Conclusions agree: "To date, no
   outcrop area of the ORS examined can be shown to have the same suite of
   lithological characteristics as documented from the Altar Stone", and
   other ORS areas "can all so far be excluded when all aspects of the
   multi-proxy analysis are considered".

10. "Six tonnes, from a spot no one has pinned down, by a route no one knows.
   Sea, sledge or ice: how do you think it got there?"
   A recap of claims 1, 3 and 4-7; it adds no new fact. "Six tonnes": claim 3.
   "A spot no one has pinned down": claim 1 (Pirrie 2026, "the exact
   provenance of the Altar Stone is as yet unknown"; Clarke 2026, "its source
   location within this large basin remains unresolved"). "A route no one
   knows": the three papers that discuss transport propose different means and
   none claims to have shown which was used. Clarke 2024 suggests the sea but
   names no route (claim 4); Parker Pearson 2024 doubts the boats of the time
   and estimates the overland haul (claims 5-6); Clarke 2026 allows ice for
   part of the way at most (claim 7). "Sea, sledge or ice" are exactly those
   three proposals. The closing question asks for the viewer's view; it is not
   a "comment X" prompt (E2) and states nothing as fact.
