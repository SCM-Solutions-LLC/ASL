In 2024, geologists traced Stonehenge's six-tonne Altar Stone to Scotland, more than seven hundred kilometres away.
The ages of tiny mineral grains inside it matched sandstone in the northeast.
So how did it get here?
The geologists suggested it went by sea.
But no late Stone Age boat has been found in Britain, and another study doubts one could carry six tonnes.
Overland, on a sledge, that study estimates about eight months of hauling.
A glacier? A 2026 ice model says ice could have carried it part of the way, at most.
Then, months later, a new study, including three of the same geologists, looked closer.
Many outcrops in that part of Scotland could not have been the source. No outcrop examined so far is a match.
Where exactly the Altar Stone came from is, for now, unknown.

The two 2026 papers disagree about how strong the Scottish match is. Clarke
2026 calls Caithness sandstone "the closest match"; Pirrie 2026 says "the exact
provenance of the Altar Stone is as yet unknown". Per GUARDRAILS A3 the script
makes only the claim both support: the exact source is not known. Clarke 2026
agrees on that point: "its source location within this large basin remains
unresolved". The script does not say the stone is not from Scotland, which
neither paper claims.

1. "In 2024, geologists traced Stonehenge's six-tonne Altar Stone to Scotland,
   more than seven hundred kilometres away."
   The hook (A4). It describes a 2024 result, so a viewer who swipes away here
   leaves with a true statement: a 2024 study did trace the stone to Scotland.
   Clarke 2024, abstract: "Detrital age comparisons ... reveal a remarkable
   similarity to the Old Red Sandstone of the Orcadian Basin in northeast
   Scotland. Such a provenance implies that the Altar Stone, a 6 tonne shaped
   block, was sourced at least 750 km from its current location." The authors
   are geologists and geochronologists (Curtin University's Timescales of
   Mineral Systems group, the National Museum Wales, and others). "More than
   seven hundred" sits under Clarke 2024's "at least 750 km". Clarke 2026 gives
   a rounder "some 700 km", which the script does not contradict. Not said: the
   name "Altar Stone" is conventional (Stone 80). No one knows it was an altar.

2. "The ages of tiny mineral grains inside it matched sandstone in the
   northeast."
   Clarke 2024: "we present in situ U-Pb, Lu-Hf and trace element isotopic data
   for zircon, apatite and rutile from two fragments of the Altar Stone"; the
   grains are "typically 10-40 µm wide"; abstract as in claim 1 ("northeast
   Scotland").

3. "So how did it get here?" A question, no claim. "When" is also unknown:
   Parker Pearson 2024, "Just when the Altar Stone arrived at Stonehenge is
   unknown"; Pirrie 2026 abstract, "Its purpose at the monument, and when it
   was transported to the site, is unknown". So the script gives no date for
   the journey.

4. "The geologists suggested it went by sea."
   Clarke 2024, abstract: "The difficulty of long-distance overland transport
   of such massive cargo from Scotland, navigating topographic barriers,
   suggests that it was transported by sea."

5. "But no late Stone Age boat has been found in Britain, and another study
   doubts one could carry six tonnes."
   Parker Pearson 2024, "How was the Altar Stone transported?": "Although no
   boats are known from the Late Neolithic in Britain or Europe ..."; "The
   earliest known boats in Britain have been found ... at Ferriby. The earliest
   of these Early Bronze Age sewn-plank boats (Ferriby 3) dates to 2030-1780
   cal bc"; "What we know of Early Bronze Age boats ... would suggest that they
   and their Late Neolithic precursors were unlikely to be sturdy enough to
   carry a 6-tonne stone in coastal waters." "One" = a boat of that period
   (the Early Bronze Age boats "and their Late Neolithic precursors"). "Late Stone Age" is the plain
   rendering of "Late Neolithic" (Neolithic = New Stone Age). "Doubts" matches
   "unlikely". "Another study" is accurate as a description of the paper, but
   note that three of its six authors (Bevins, Ixer, Pearce) also wrote Clarke
   2024.

6. "Overland, on a sledge, that study estimates about eight months of
   hauling."
   Parker Pearson 2024: "necessitating the use of a suitably robust wooden
   sledge ... sliding on wooden 'rails'"; "such a journey with a large and
   heavy sledge could take perhaps four times today's time of under two months
   ... needing eight months or so to haul a stone from northern Scotland to
   Stonehenge." "About" stands for "perhaps ... or so": it is an estimate, and
   the script says so.

7. "A glacier? A 2026 ice model says ice could have carried it part of the
   way, at most."
   Clarke 2026, abstract: "we combine sandstone provenance analysis with ice
   flow modelling"; "Glacial dispersal to Dogger Bank would reduce the
   anthropogenic transport distance required to Stonehenge from 700 km to 400
   km"; "Glacial transport may have provided an intermediate stage in the
   stone's journey, but alone cannot account for the final emplacement on
   Salisbury Plain. Even under a glacially assisted scenario, substantial
   anthropogenic transport would have remained necessary." "Part of the way, at
   most" = "may have provided an intermediate stage ... alone cannot account".
   The abstract adds a further problem (Dogger Bank
   was flooded before the stone likely arrived); the script leaves that out
   because it only weakens the glacier idea further. Parker Pearson 2024 goes
   further, "Clarke et al. (2024) have discussed why theories involving Ice Age
   glacial transport can be rejected", but the script uses the narrower 2026
   wording, which is based on an actual ice model.

8. "Then, months later, a new study, including three of the same geologists,
   looked closer."
   Pirrie 2026 (November 2026) came about five months after Clarke 2026 (4 June
   2026). Its authors include Bevins, Ixer and Pearce, all three of whom are
   authors of Clarke 2024. "Looked closer" stands for the abstract's "detailed
   multi-proxy characterization of the sedimentology, geochemistry, detrital
   and diagenetic mineralogy of the Altar Stone".

9. "Many outcrops in that part of Scotland could not have been the source. No
   outcrop examined so far is a match."
   Pirrie 2026, abstract: "the overall petrology, heavy mineral assemblage, clay
   mineralogy and geochemistry show that many outcrops of the Old Red Sandstone
   (ORS) in the Orcadian Basin could not have been the source of the Altar
   Stone. To date, no examined outcrop area of the UK ORS can be shown to have
   the same suite of lithological and geochemical characteristics as
   documented for the Altar Stone." "That part of Scotland" = the Orcadian
   Basin of northeast Scotland (claim 2). The same abstract says the earlier
   grain dating "supports a Laurentian source for the heavy minerals", so it
   narrows the source down rather than overturning the 2024 result. The script
   says only what is above.

10. "Where exactly the Altar Stone came from is, for now, unknown."
    Pirrie 2026, abstract: "this integrated dataset shows that the exact
    provenance of the Altar Stone is as yet unknown." Clarke 2026, abstract:
    "its source location within this large basin remains unresolved". Both
    support it.
