Sort the cells in your body by size, from red blood cells up, and something odd turns up: each size class weighs roughly the same.
A twenty twenty-three study combined over fifteen hundred published sources to estimate the body's cells, type by type, and sorted them by size.
The smallest cells it sorted are red blood cells.
The biggest are muscle fibres, more than a million times heavier.
Now put those cells into size classes, each class twice as heavy as the one before.
Each time the size doubles, there are, on average, about half as many cells.
So the small cells win on numbers, the big cells win on size, and the two cancel out.
Every class ends up holding roughly the same weight.
With one exception: a dip, between the fat cells and the muscle fibres.
In a typical woman the dip is smaller, and in a typical ten-year-old it is fainter still.
Take a typical man. His muscle fibres are fewer than one in fifty thousand of his cells with a nucleus.
Yet they make up about half the weight of all his cells.
And all his cells together weigh about forty-five kilograms.
The other twenty-five of his seventy are not cells at all. They are the water, protein and minerals between them.
Sort the life in the ocean by size, as it was before industrial fishing, and most size classes held about the same weight too.
And in your body, nobody knows why. No accepted process explains it yet.
Trillions of tiny cells, far fewer giant ones, and roughly the same weight at every size.

SOURCES (for approval — every claim in the script, with the number behind it)

The longer follow-up to script/cell-count.txt. Its core claim is new (B1): the cell-count short
is about how many cells are human and which type dominates the count; this one is about how cell
mass is spread across cell sizes. Publish one of the two, not both.

Sources:
- Hatton, Galbraith, Merleau, Miettinen, Smith & Shander, "The human cell count
  and size distribution", PNAS 120(39): e2303077120 (2023),
  doi:10.1073/pnas.2303077120; open full text at PMC10523466. Read by the author
  of this script, 19 Nov 2026. Reference male 70 kg, reference female 60 kg,
  reference child 10 years, 32 kg.
- Hatton, Heneghan, Bar-On & Galbraith, "The global ocean size spectrum from
  bacteria to whales", Science Advances 7(46): eabh3732 (2021),
  doi:10.1126/sciadv.abh3732; open full text at PMC8580314. Cited by the PNAS
  paper as another example of the same pattern.

1. "Sort the cells in your body by size, from red blood cells up, and something odd turns up: each size class weighs roughly the same."
   Hatton 2023: "a roughly similar fraction of total body mass is present in each
   logarithmic cell-size class" (Fig. 4A), and the female and child patterns are
   "broadly similar to the male". "Roughly" is the paper's word and it carries
   weight: outside the dip (line 9), "all variation in cellular biomass lies
   within two orders of magnitude". The hook says "roughly", not "exactly".
   "Size" here is cell mass in grams, the paper's measure of size. "From red blood
   cells up", not "every cell": the binning leaves out platelets and the smallest
   haploid gametes (line 3), so the hook names where the sorting starts.

2. "A twenty twenty-three study combined over fifteen hundred published sources to estimate the body's cells, type by type, and sorted them by size."
   "Estimate", not "count": the paper compiles published data and says "we estimate".
   "Published sources", not "studies": they include reference catalogues such as
   ICRP's reference man.
   Hatton 2023, PNAS, online 18 Sep 2023: counts and sizes for 400 cell types
   across 60 tissue systems (1,264 cell groups), from ">1,500 published
   sources". Not
   called the "first" census: Sender & Milo and others counted before it.

3. "The smallest cells it sorted are red blood cells."
   Hatton 2023: the binned distribution "only considers the distribution beginning
   at red blood cells (4 × 10^-11 g)", i.e. "beginning at the lower bound of
   erythrocytes". Platelets and the smallest haploid gametes are smaller but were
   left out of the binning "to avoid fitting the distribution over relatively large
   gaps", hence "the smallest cells it sorted". 4e-11 g is the lower bound of red
   blood cells, not a typical one, so no red-cell mass is spoken; the axis starts
   there and says "from 4 × 10⁻¹¹ g".

4. "The biggest are muscle fibres, more than a million times heavier."
   Hatton 2023: cell sizes run "from red blood cells to the largest muscle
   fibers"; a myocyte is "more than a million-fold larger" than a red blood
   cell; myocyte biomass peaks "near 10^-4 g" (10^-4 / 4e-11 = 2.5 million). A floor,
   so the card says ">1,000,000×": the full range is seven orders of magnitude.

5. "Now put those cells into size classes, each class twice as heavy as the one before."
   Hatton 2023, Methods: "bins increase by a multiplicative factor of 2 from small
   to large (octave size classes)". "Those cells": the ones from red blood cells
   up; "We ignored binning cells smaller than erythrocytes".

6. "Each time the size doubles, there are, on average, about half as many cells."
   Hatton 2023: the count per size class follows "a power law with a slope near
   -0.97 with a 95% CI of -1.09 to -0.87". A slope of -1 means half as many cells
   per doubling. "On average" because it is a fitted trend, not true bin by bin.

7. "So the small cells win on numbers, the big cells win on size, and the two cancel out."
   Arithmetic from line 6: count times size is about constant when count falls as
   1/size.

8. "Every class ends up holding roughly the same weight."
   As line 1.

9. "With one exception: a dip, between the fat cells and the muscle fibres."
   Hatton 2023: "prominent biomass peaks comprising adipocytes (near 10^-6 g) and
   myocytes (near 10^-4 g), separated by a gap of relatively low biomass".

10. "In a typical woman the dip is smaller, and in a typical ten-year-old it is fainter still."
   Hatton 2023, Fig. 4B: the gap is "less prominent in the reference female" and
   "even less evident in the 10-y-old reference child". "Typical": these are the
   paper's 60 kg reference woman and 32 kg reference child, models scaled from the
   reference man, and fat and muscle vary widely between people. On screen this is
   text only (no drawn rows): the paper compares the dip across the three bodies on
   a linear axis, which a schematic row would misstate.

11. "Take a typical man. His muscle fibres are fewer than one in fifty thousand of his cells with a nucleus."
   Hatton 2023: skeletal myocytes "make up <0.002% of the nucleated cell count"
   (reference male). 0.002% = 1 in 50,000, an upper limit, so the card says "< 1 IN
   50,000" and the dot grid "FEWER THAN 1 MUSCLE FIBRE".

12. "Yet they make up about half the weight of all his cells."
   Hatton 2023, Fig. 1 caption: "Cell biomass is dominated by skeletal myocytes,
   comprising about half of all 45 kg of cell biomass in the body".

13. "And all his cells together weigh about forty-five kilograms."
   As line 12: 45 kg of cell biomass, reference male.

14. "The other twenty-five of his seventy are not cells at all. They are the water, protein and minerals between them."
   Hatton 2023, Fig. 2 caption: cell biomass "excludes noncellular components of
   biomass in each tissue, made up of extracellular water, protein, and minerals
   (~25 kg)". 45 + 25 = 70 kg, the reference male.

15. "Sort the life in the ocean by size, as it was before industrial fishing, and most size classes held about the same weight too."
   Hatton 2021: "biomass within most order of magnitude size classes is indeed
   remarkably constant, near 1 gigatonne (Gt) wet weight (10^15 g), but bacteria
   and large marine mammals are markedly above and below this value,
   respectively." Hence "most". That holds for the ocean before industrial
   fishing (before 1850); today "the upper one-third of the biomass spectrum has
   been severely truncated", hence the past tense and the qualifier, also on the
   chart's title. Its classes are tenfold, not twofold; the line does not claim the
   same class width.

16. "And in your body, nobody knows why. No accepted process explains it yet."
   Hatton 2023, Discussion: "To our knowledge, there is no universally recognized
   process that accounts for these patterns." The paper then proposes candidates
   (random growth processes; growth and division rates out of step), so the line
   says no accepted process, not none at all. "And", not "but": the ocean paper
   does not claim its pattern is explained either.

17. "Trillions of tiny cells, far fewer giant ones, and roughly the same weight at every size."
   Summary of lines 3-8. "Trillions": red blood cells are most of the ≈36
   trillion cells of the reference male (Hatton 2023). "At every size" means
   every size class, with the dip of line 9; "roughly" carries the spread of line 1.

18. The size chart, not spoken.
   A log-scale size axis from 10^-11 to 10^-2 g, labelled "log scale", with the
   paper's 26 doubling classes from 4e-11 g (to about 2.7e-3 g; Fig. 3, Methods). The falling
   count staircase and the mass bars are drawn as a schematic of the trend in
   lines 6-9 and say "schematic" on screen; they are not the paper's per-bin data.
   The single weight chart is the reference man. The ocean card raises the first
   three classes (bacteria, about 2.5, 2.5 and 1.9 Gt against about 1 Gt) and lowers
   the last two (the mammal classes), as Hatton 2021 Fig. 2 does.
   Only the axis positions of red blood cells (4e-11 g), fat cells (~1e-6 g) and
   muscle fibres (~1e-4 g) are values from the paper.
