By the best cell counts we have, a typical man is about half human.
About thirty trillion of his cells are his own.
About thirty-eight trillion are bacteria.
But put them all on a scale, and those bacteria weigh about two hundred grams.
And your own cells are not what you picture. Most of them are red blood cells.
By count, your cells are mostly blood. And his passengers roughly match them, cell for cell.

SOURCES (for approval — every claim in the script, with the number behind it)

Two peer-reviewed counts, read in full by an independent fact-check (18 Nov 2026):
- Sender, Fuchs & Milo, "Revised Estimates for the Number of Human and Bacteria
  Cells in the Body", PLoS Biology 14(8): e1002533 (2016),
  doi:10.1371/journal.pbio.1002533. For a "reference man" (20-30 years old,
  70 kg, 170 cm): 3.0e13 human cells (2% uncertainty, which the authors say
  "might be too optimistically low"), 3.8e13 bacteria, a ratio B/H = 1.3 with
  25% uncertainty, which they call "about 1:1". Of the two counts cited, the
  only one that pairs human cells and bacteria for the same reference body,
  which is why its 30 and 38 trillion are the numbers spoken.
- Hatton et al., PNAS 120(39): e2303077120 (2023), doi:10.1073/pnas.2303077120:
  "≈36 trillion cells in the male" (70 kg, 176 cm, 20-50 y), "≈28 trillion in
  the female"; earlier studies "converging on values of 30 to 37 trillion human
  cells"; it cites Sender for "an approximately equal number of bacteria cells".

The spoken line was narrowed so it is true on both counts: 30 of 68 trillion is
44% human (Sender), 36 of 74 is 49% (Hatton with Sender's bacteria). Sender's
reference woman is about a third human (21 and 44 trillion), so the screen
names the body the numbers are for ("typical 70 kg man"), and the voice
keeps to "his" for every count. The lines about red blood cells ("your own
cells", "mostly blood") hold for any body in either paper, so they say "your".

1. "By the best cell counts we have, a typical man is about half human."
   44% (Sender) or 49% (Hatton) for the 70 kg reference man. The line names the
   body because the answer depends on it: Sender's reference woman is about a
   third human (21 and 44 trillion), and its other groups 30-42%. The hook card,
   the first card of the cutout and the title name "typical (70 kg) man" too.
   The short does not claim bacteria outnumber human cells.
   The count treats red blood cells as cells, as both papers do. Sender notes
   that against nucleated human cells only, bacteria outnumber them about
   10:1 ("a question of definition"). The short never captions the 10:1 figure.
   The paper counts microbial cells and "operationally" calls them bacteria;
   archaea and eukaryotes are 2-3 orders of magnitude fewer.

2. "About thirty trillion of his cells are his own."
   3.0e13 (Sender); Hatton's 36 trillion is within "about thirty" at A3's limit,
   and on screen, in both looks, the 30 trillion is marked as the 2016 count.

3. "About thirty-eight trillion are bacteria."
   3.8e13 (Sender), nearly all in the colon. Sender treats its colon estimate as
   "in some respects ... an upper limit". The animation scatters them through
   the figure because it is a count, not a map.

4. "Those bacteria weigh about two hundred grams."
   Total bacterial mass ~0.2 kg (Sender). They are that light because a
   bacterium is on average far smaller than a human cell; the screen says "far
   smaller on average". Sender revises earlier statements of 1-3 kg (1-3% of
   body mass), citing a 2012 NIH news release.

5. "Most of them are red blood cells."
   Sender: "The cell count is dominated by red blood cells (84%)", 2.5e13 of
   3.0e13. Hatton: non-nucleated blood cells (red cells and platelets) ≈29 of
   ≈36 trillion. "Most" holds on both; the 84% figure is not spoken.

6. "By count, your cells are mostly blood."
   True by count only, and the line says so. By MASS it is the other way round:
   "75% of total cell mass is composed of two cell types, fat cells
   (adipocytes) and muscle cells (myocytes)" (Sender; Hatton agrees). Red blood
   cells are "among the smallest cell types in the human body" (Sender).

7. "And his passengers roughly match them, cell for cell."
   For the typical man of the hook: Sender's "about 1:1" (1.3 with 25%
   uncertainty); 38 against 30-36 trillion.

8. The unit chart, not spoken.
   68 dots, one per trillion cells: 30 human (25 of them red blood cells) and 38
   bacteria, Sender's rounded figures, labelled "(2016 count)" where the human
   total is shown.
