The deepest hole humans have ever dug was finished with a bit narrower than a dinner plate.
The Soviet Union started drilling it in May nineteen seventy, on the Kola Peninsula.
At the top, the hole was seventy-two centimetres wide.
It was drilled in steps, each narrower than the last, down to a bit just twenty-one centimetres across.
By nineteen seventy-five, it was over seven kilometres deep.
In nineteen seventy-nine, at nine and a half kilometres, it beat the American record.
By the end of nineteen eighty-three, it had reached twelve kilometres.
Then, in nineteen eighty-four, the drill string broke, and they had to start again from seven kilometres down.
By nineteen ninety, the new branch had reached twelve thousand, two hundred and sixty-two metres.
No hole has gone deeper since.
The rock at the bottom was expected to be around a hundred and twenty degrees.
At ten kilometres, it was already a hundred and eighty.
At the bottom, it was over two hundred.
Drilling stopped in nineteen ninety-two, when its funding was cut. The plan had been to go on to thirteen kilometres.
One recent paper adds that the heat made the rock flow and close up the hole.
And for all that, it never made it through the crust: it got about a third of the way.
Twelve kilometres down. The centre of the Earth is over six thousand more.

Sources read (an independent fact-check, 18 Nov 2026):
- Lobanov, Chicherov & Sharov, Arktika i Sever 44: 267-284 (2021),
  doi:10.37482/issn2221-2698.2021.44.267. Russian Academy of Sciences authors;
  full text. "Drilling was started on May 24, 1970"; "In 1990, the maximum depth
  was reached - 12262 m"; a "214 mm drill bit"; "at a depth of 10 km reached
  180°C, and at a depth of about 12 km ... 212°C, instead of the assumed
  120°C"; "a drastic reduction in funding made it impossible to continue the
  work. Drilling was stopped in 1992". Fig. 5 (actual well construction) and the
  depth-against-year account behind lines 3-9.
- Zhu & Huang, Communications Earth & Environment 7: 124 (2026),
  doi:10.1038/s43247-026-03246-z: "reaching 12,262 m between 1970 and 1989";
  "temperatures reached 180 °C at 10 km and 212 °C at 12 km"; "Drilling was
  abandoned in 1992 when unexpectedly high bottom-hole temperatures of 212 °C
  induced plastic rock flow that repeatedly sealed the hole."
- Clarke et al., "The Kola Superdeep Drill Hole by Ye. A. Kozlovskiy (1984): A
  detailed summary", USGS Open-File Report 86-517 (1986), summarising
  Kozlovskiy, Kol'skaya sverkhglubokaya (Moscow: Nedra, 1984): "Crustal
  thickness in the vicinity of the corehole was shown to be about 40 km".
- NASA NSSDC Earth Fact Sheet: volumetric mean radius 6,371 km; core radius

1. "The deepest hole humans have ever dug was finished with a bit narrower than a dinner plate."
   As script/deepest-hole.txt items 1-2: Kola Superdeep Borehole SG-3, 12,262 m, "which has not
   been surpassed so far" (Lobanov 2021); finished with a 214 mm bit (Lobanov), against a dinner
   plate 25.6 cm across (Met 51.86.100). "Deepest" (vertical depth), never "longest".

2. "The Soviet Union started drilling it in May nineteen seventy, on the Kola Peninsula."
   "Drilling was started on May 24, 1970" (Lobanov); Kola Peninsula, USSR (Lobanov).

3. "At the top, the hole was seventy-two centimetres wide."
   Lobanov Fig. 5: 720 mm to 40 m.

4. "It was drilled in steps, each narrower than the last, down to a bit just twenty-one centimetres across."
   Lobanov Fig. 5: 720 mm to 40 m; cased at 325 mm to 2,000 m; cased at 245 mm to 8,770 m; open
   hole 215.9 mm to 12,262 m, drilled with a 214 mm bit (21.4 cm, said as twenty-one). Lobanov
   also has the bore going oval below 7 km with "many caverns", so the steps are the design, not
   a measured width at every depth; the screen draws the design and says so.

5. "By nineteen seventy-five, it was over seven kilometres deep."
   7,263 m in May 1975 (Lobanov).

6. "In nineteen seventy-nine, at nine and a half kilometres, it beat the American record."
   9,584 m in June 1979, passing the US record of 9,583 m (Lobanov). 9.58 km said as "nine and a
   half".

7. "By the end of nineteen eighty-three, it had reached twelve kilometres."
   12,000 m on 27 December 1983 (Lobanov).

8. "Then, in nineteen eighty-four, the drill string broke, and they had to start again from seven kilometres down."
   12,066 m before the 1984 drill-string break, which sent drilling back to 7 km; the record branch
   was sidetracked at 7,010 m (Lobanov).

9. "By nineteen ninety, the new branch had reached twelve thousand, two hundred and sixty-two metres."
   12,262 m: 1990 in Lobanov, 1989 in Zhu & Huang. "By 1990" is true on either reading.

10. "No hole has gone deeper since."
   "Which has not been surpassed so far" (Lobanov 2021); Zhu & Huang (2026) still treat it as the
   deepest.

11. "The rock at the bottom was expected to be around a hundred and twenty degrees."
   "Instead of the assumed 120°C" (Lobanov). Celsius; the screen says so, the voice does not.

12. "At ten kilometres, it was already a hundred and eighty."
   180 °C at 10 km (Lobanov; Zhu & Huang).

13. "At the bottom, it was over two hundred."
   212 °C at about 12 km (Lobanov; Zhu & Huang).

14. "Drilling stopped in nineteen ninety-two, when its funding was cut. The plan had been to go on to thirteen kilometres."
   "A drastic reduction in funding made it impossible to continue the work. Drilling was stopped
   in 1992" (Lobanov); a casing run in 1992 was meant to carry drilling on to 13 km (Lobanov).

15. "One recent paper adds that the heat made the rock flow and close up the hole."
   Zhu & Huang (2026): 212 °C "induced plastic rock flow that repeatedly sealed the hole". That
   sentence carries no citation of its own, and Lobanov does not mention plastic flow, so the
   line attributes it to one paper rather than stating it (A3, A5).

16. "And for all that, it never made it through the crust: it got about a third of the way."
   Crust about 40 km thick at the site (Kozlovskiy, USGS OFR 86-517): 12.262 / 40 = 31%.

17. "Twelve kilometres down. The centre of the Earth is over six thousand more."
   Mean radius 6,371 km (NSSDC); 6,371 - 12.262 = 6,359 km to go: "over six thousand more".
