Two archaeologists say Easter Island's platform statues were reshaped after they arrived.
Dozens lie along the old roads from the quarry, apparently dropped on the way.
Those statues have bases almost as wide as their shoulders, and they lean forward.
Rocked side to side with ropes, a statue like that steps forward.
In a test, eighteen people walked a scaled-down copy weighing over four tonnes a hundred meters in forty minutes.
The statues on the platforms stand straight, on bases narrower than their shoulders.
Eye sockets were carved only once a statue stood on its platform. None of the stranded ones have them.
So, they argue, the shape built for walking was carved away at the end of the journey.
Other archaeologists reject this. Some think many road statues were set up there on purpose.

SOURCES (for approval — every claim in the script, with the number behind it)

Topic chosen by the owner on 2026-11-24 after a B2 check: the plain "the moai
walked, not rolled on logs" fills the first page of search results, so this
short leads with the less-told consequence (the statues were reshaped on
arrival). C6: Rapa Nui heritage. The script attributes the reading, names the
disagreement (entry 9) and mocks no one.

Primary paper, full text read on 2026-11-24 (the publisher's PDF as hosted by
newswise.com, fetched through the Firecrawl scraper because this sandbox cannot
reach it directly): Lipo & Hunt, "The walking moai hypothesis: Archaeological
evidence, experimental validation, and response to critics", Journal of
Archaeological Science 183, 106383 (2025), doi:10.1016/j.jas.2025.106383.
Called "LH25" below. The authors are the main proponents of walking transport;
entry 9 records the published opposition, which LH25 itself lists.

1. "Two archaeologists say Easter Island's platform statues were reshaped after
   they arrived."
   LH25 1.1: on road moai the base nearly equals the shoulder width; "platform
   statues show ratios consistently exceeding 1.0, with shoulders wider than
   bases ... This modification occurred after transport, when carvers reduced
   the base width". And: "Platform moai, once they reached their destinations,
   were substantially modified through base reshaping to eliminate this lean.
   Carvers removed material from the front of the base". 2.4: "These statues
   were later modified to stand erect on ceremonial platforms". The hook
   attributes this to the two authors ("two archaeologists say"), because it is
   their reading and entry 9 records the opposition. It says "platform statues",
   not every standing statue: the standing statues on the slopes of Rano Raraku
   never left the quarry, and LH25 claims reshaping only for ahu moai. The
   paper's only direct physical evidence for the trimming is "Tailings of Rano
   Raraku volcanic tuff from moai and found in ahu platforms" (S. Rapu, personal
   communication, 2011); the rest is the base-ratio and lean comparison in
   entries 3 and 7. LH25 Conclusion notes small moai (under 2 m) may have been
   moved differently; the script makes no claim about them.

2. "Dozens lie along the old roads from the quarry, apparently dropped on the
   way."
   LH25 abstract and 1.1: "62 statues documented along ancient transport roads,
   abandoned where they fell at various points of their journey from the Rano
   Raraku quarry"; roads "radiate from Rano Raraku". The count differs inside
   LH25: 62 in the abstract and text, "84 road moai" in the Fig. 12 caption. The
   script says "dozens", which both numbers satisfy (A3). "Apparently dropped"
   marks the transport-failure reading as the authors', not a settled fact
   (entry 9).

3. "Those statues have bases almost as wide as their shoulders, and they lean
   forward."
   LH25 1.1: "road statues exhibit a shoulder-to-base width ratio approaching
   1.0, meaning the base width nearly equals the shoulder width"; "road moai
   exhibit a pronounced and consistent forward lean, typically ranging from 6°
   to 15° from vertical". The script gives no angle, because LH25 also gives
   5–15° (Fig. 1) and ~5–14° (Fig. 5).

4. Cut after the first fact-check: "A statue like that can't stand up on its
   own." LH25 1.1 says the lean "renders independent standing impossible without
   support", but its own Fig. 4 shows the centre of mass of moai 12-220-01 (the
   statue the test replica copies) "projected within the base footprint". The
   source contradicts itself, so the script makes no claim either way.

5. "Rocked side to side with ropes, a statue like that steps forward."
   LH25 1.1: "Each lateral rocking motion thus produces a forward 'step'";
   Fig. 1: "alternating lateral rope pulls ... A restraining rope prevents
   forward toppling as side ropes create a controlled rocking motion that
   advances the statue with each oscillation."

6. "In a test, eighteen people walked a scaled-down copy weighing over four
   tonnes a hundred meters in forty minutes."
   "Scaled-down": LH25 1.4, the original road moai 12-220-01 "stands 7.35 m tall
   and 2.83 m wide. Our concrete replica, scaled for practical experimentation,
   weighed 4.35 metric tons". Without it, a viewer would take 4 t and 18 people
   as what a full-size statue needed.
   LH25 abstract: "a precisely scaled 4.35 metric ton replica based on road moai
   morphology ... covering 100 m in 40 min with a team of 18 people". 1.4:
   "With a minimum crew of 18 individuals, four on each lateral rope and ten on
   the rear rope". 4.35 t = "over four tonnes". The script gives no year: Fig. 8
   dates the demonstration to 2012, but the text does not tie the 100 m figure
   to a single dated trial.

7. "The statues on the platforms stand straight, on bases narrower than their
   shoulders."
   LH25 1.1: platform statues show shoulder-to-base ratios "consistently
   exceeding 1.0, with shoulders wider than bases"; Fig. 2: the ahu and road
   ratios are "statistically distinctive (t = 2.474, df = 86.0, p = 0.0153)",
   from Van Tilburg's (1986) measurements. 2.4: "ahu moai ... stand vertically
   upright (bases flattened) with their center of mass positioned stably over
   their base".

8. "Eye sockets were carved only once a statue stood on its platform. None of
   the stranded ones have them."
   LH25 1.1: "Ethnographic accounts and archaeological evidence confirm that
   eyes were carved only as a final step after the statue's successful erection
   on the platform"; "The complete absence of eye sockets on all 62 documented
   road moai confirms these statues never reached the ritual completion stage."
   The script does not say every platform statue has eye sockets; LH25 does not
   claim that.

9. "So, they argue, the shape built for walking was carved away at the end of
   the journey. Other archaeologists reject this. Some think many road statues
   were set up there on purpose."
   LH25 2.4: "Once the statues reached their destinations, these transport
   features were carved away to create stable, upright monuments." "They argue"
   keeps it the authors' conclusion, including the "built for walking" premise.
   "Other archaeologists reject this" covers the scholars LH25 1 says "continue
   to reject the walking hypothesis"; "Some think" is the narrower ritual-path
   reading below, held by only some of them.
   The disagreement, as LH25 2.5 reports it: "Richards et al. (2011), Seager
   Thomas et al. (2023), and Cauwe and De Dapper (2022, 2015) ... [argue] that
   these statues were deliberately positioned along ceremonial paths leading to
   the 'sacred center' of Rano Raraku rather than representing transport
   failures"; Routledge (1919) and Heyerdahl (1989) held a similar view. LH25
   1: "some scholars continue to reject the walking hypothesis" and names
   "Bahn, Flenley, Van Tilburg, Diamond, Velasco, Richards, Cauwe, and De
   Dapper". These critics were read only as LH25 quotes them (A2), so the line
   states only their position, not their evidence. "Many road statues" rather
   than "all": Cauwe and De Dapper speak of "isolated" moai, and LH25 does not
   say every critic holds the view for every statue.

Not used: oral tradition that the moai "walked" (LH25 cites it via Métraux
1940; not read at source, and it is the top-result framing); the Pavel 1986
experiment; transport-rate and workforce models; the deforestation argument
(a second claim, B1).
