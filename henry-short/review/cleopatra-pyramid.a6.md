# cleopatra-pyramid — A6 check of everything on screen (VO af_heart 1.0, 65.58 s; opens on "Not this")

Each on-screen string or picture, and the SOURCES item in `script/cleopatra-pyramid.txt` it rests on.
Script edited 2026-11-22 (new hook and closing question) and 2026-11-23 (the sentence on the workers' vegetables, laxatives and talents cut as off-topic and unclear; "papyrus documents"; "an inspector named Merer", the word of Tallet & Marouard 2014 p. 9; owner's decisions); A7 re-run: `factcheck/cleopatra-pyramid.json`, gate `node tools/check-factcheck.mjs cleopatra-pyramid`.

Rebuilt 2026-11-22/23 as one 3D world (`tools/blender/cleopatra_world.py` → `public/3d/cleopatra-world.mp4`): after the photo, one world (Giza, the Nile, Tura, the Red Sea harbour) shown as separate shots, each with a slow drift, joined by half-second cross-dissolves: fast flights between places were overwhelming and hard cuts were choppy (owner, 2026-11-23). The dissolve uses a second clip, `public/3d/cleopatra-world-b.mp4`, holding the incoming shot around each cut. Labels, counters and brackets are Remotion overlays pinned to the 3D objects (`src/generated/cleopatra-world.anchors.json`). Shapes are procedural; surfaces use CC0 scans from Poly Haven, and the people are MakeHuman bodies (CC0) posed and dressed by `tools/blender/make_people.py`; all credited in `public/SOURCES.json`.

**People** are generic and nobody is a portrait: body shapes come from MakeHuman's sliders, skin is tinted to a range of browns as a neutral choice for an illustration and not a claim about any person (C6), and the one named figure, Diodorus, is shown only as hands, a pen and a page, never a face (A5). Every other figure stays about 8 m or more away. Dress was not sourced when it was modelled; A7 pass 4 checked it against sources it opened itself: workers' white linen kilts, short hair and brown skin (Erman, *Life in Ancient Egypt*, 1894, ch. II and X), rowers facing the stern (Erman ch. XIX), Diodorus's tunic and brimmed hat (Smith, *Dictionary of Greek and Roman Antiquities*, 1875, s.v. Tunica, Pileus), and his sandals (s.v. Calceus: shoes "used in walking out of doors or in travelling"; A7 pass 5 flagged him barefoot). These are that pass's readings, not re-read here.

**The timeline** is a line paved in pale stone on the desert floor, with a drawn gold line over it so it reads against the sand, running west from the pyramid's foot: distance along the ground = years (0.25 m per year, one linear scale). The first version drew time as depth in a cut through the ground; it was dropped because it read as sand burying things. Where two spans are compared, the camera looks straight down (4° off vertical), so both are drawn to one scale and perspective cannot flatter the claim. The paving and its markers are shown only in the two straight-down views and in the closing shot; they are hidden everywhere else, so they cannot be read as ancient structures or as a road (A7 pass 3; owner's decision 2026-11-23, after the dark version read as an asphalt road).

**Cleopatra** is shown once, by a real photo that fills the frame while she is first named: the marble head in the Antikensammlung Berlin (Altes Museum), the "Berlin Cleopatra", inv. 1976.10. Its date is given differently: the museum's catalogue record (recherche.smb.museum/detail/677025) says third quarter of the 1st century BC, "um 50 – 38 v. Chr."; the Commons description says ca. 40-30 BC (A3; read by A7 pass 6). Either way it was made in her lifetime (69-30 BC); on screen it says only "1st century BC". Photo by Louis le Grand, released into the public domain on Wikimedia Commons; credited on screen anyway; declared in `public/SOURCES.json`. The identification is the museum's (by likeness to her coins, not an inscription), not re-derived here. No face is modelled for her: that would be an invented likeness (A5). She is not shown as a child where the script says "when Cleopatra was a girl"; that line carries only the text "Cleopatra: about 9-13".

Not in the script, so cited here (A1-A2): the pyramid and the scale figure are modelled to **Petrie, *The Pyramids and Temples of Gizeh* (1883), sec. 25, p. 43**: "The mean base being 9068.8 ± .5 inches" (230.35 m; the metric conversion is ours). Casing joints drawn as fine lines: **sec. 26, p. 44**, "the mean opening of the joint was but 1/50 inch". Read in R. Birdsall's transcription (ronaldbirdsall.com/gizeh/petrie/c6.html), not the scanned original. No size is stated on screen; the person is there only to make it legible. Because a person stands beside the masonry, its course heights are a claim too: **sec. 32** says "the casing courses were on the same levels as the present core courses", so the stepped core and the casing joints share one course table, a base course of about 1.5 m (sec. 26) and about 200 courses above it drawn at one even height of 0.73 m. The even height is a simplification (Petrie's courses vary); block widths are illustrative. The height is a proportion and is never shown (A7 pass 4).

No photo of the real papyri is used: none carries a free licence (searched Wikimedia Commons, the Wikipedia articles in six languages, Openverse and the web, 2026-11-22; every published photo is credited to Pierre Tallet's mission or a press agency). The papyri are modelled and labelled "illustration". A permission request is drafted in `review/cleopatra-pyramid.photo-request.md`.
